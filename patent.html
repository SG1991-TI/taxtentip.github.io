Patent Prosecution & International Filing

Understand the journey from drafting an invention to securing protection across the globe.

What is Patent Prosecution?

A patent protects an invention by granting its owner the exclusive right to prevent others from making, using or selling that invention. Patent prosecution refers to the interaction between the applicant and a patent office during the life of a patent application. It includes both the pre‑grant stage, when the application is examined to meet patentability requirements, and the post‑grant stage, when the granted patent is maintained and challenged. Pre‑grant tasks include drafting and filing the application, responding to examiner’s objections and presenting the invention’s novelty and inventive step over existing technologies. Post‑grant prosecution involves paying maintenance fees and dealing with oppositions or amendments to keep the patent enforceable.

Pre‑grant vs. Post‑grant

  • Pre‑grant: Drafting specifications, filing applications, responding to office actions and ensuring the invention meets novelty and inventive‑step requirements.
  • Post‑grant: Maintaining the patent through renewals, handling oppositions and challenges, and making amendments to ensure enforceability throughout its term.

International Filing Routes

When you need protection in more than one country, two treaties streamline the process:

Paris Convention Route

The Paris Convention, established in 1883, allows applicants to claim a priority date across member countries. If a patent application is filed in one country, the applicant has 12 months to file in other member countries; these subsequent filings retain the original filing date. This route doesn’t provide a centralised application—each national application must still be filed and prosecuted separately—yet it ensures that foreign applicants receive the same legal treatment as nationals of each country.

PCT Route

The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) is an international system administered by WIPO that lets you file one “international” application to seek patent protection in all PCT member states. A single PCT application, filed in one language at one receiving office, has the same legal effect as filing separate national applications in each contracting state. After filing, the application undergoes an international search and is published. The applicant then decides, usually within 30 months of the priority date, which countries to enter for national‑phase examination. The PCT route buys time to evaluate the commercial potential of the invention, offers centralised feedback via the International Searching Authority and International Preliminary Examining Authority and can be more economical when many countries are involved.

Choosing a Route

Neither the Paris Convention nor the PCT grants a “global patent”—protection is ultimately obtained through national patents. The Paris route is often chosen when applicants plan to file in only a few countries and want lower upfront costs. The PCT route is better for applicants seeking protection in numerous jurisdictions or who need more time to decide where to proceed.
